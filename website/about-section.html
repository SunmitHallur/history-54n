About this project

Stanford History 54N final project: why I traced dissemblance and respectability from Jacobs and Wells to Anita Hill, #MeToo, and clinical bias.

About me

Economics major, Civil Rights minor; how class readings on Sherald, Hill, and Jacobs shaped this question.

I’m a rising senior at Stanford majoring in Economics with a minor in Civil Rights history. History 54N sits at the intersection of those interests, but what stayed with me most was a pattern I kept noticing in the readings: Black women appeared again and again as limited by their surroundings, their inner lives reduced to what the archive—or the classroom—was built to see.

Our in-class discussions started with Amy Sherald and Anita Hill, and I began to notice how Black women were systematically silenced. Speaking up often meant choosing between coming forward with your story and protecting your community, because telling the truth could mean bringing down the legacy of a successful Black man. As the weeks went on, we moved into the antebellum era, where the same failures showed up in enslaved Black women whose voices and concerns were captured far less often than those of Black men. They had to walk a fine line just to be heard, even as they endured sexual violence and physical abuse—often the same violence, or worse, that the record associated with slavery as a whole. Yet when we talked about slavery’s brutality, lynching dominated the conversation, and lynching, as we framed it, largely featured Black men.

Nell Painter’s soul murder and Harriet Jacobs’s Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl showed me how deep that silencing ran, and it pushed me toward a question I couldn’t let go: why had this happened, and how does it affect Black women today?

That question only grew louder as the quarter continued. We took up respectability politics around Anita Hill, read Tera Hunter’s To ’Joy My Freedom, discussed intersectionality through Nella Larsen’s Passing and Kimberlé Crenshaw’s TED Talk, and talked about Serena Williams—and then Drew Dixon came to class and described the same pressures in the present. The pattern was too consistent to treat as background noise; it became this project’s starting point.

Expectations and Findings

I expected respectability to burden celebrities; research showed dissemblance in hospitals, schools, and courtrooms too.

Going in, I underestimated how deep this ran. I figured the politics of respectability were mainly a burden for high-profile Black women—public figures like Anita Hill or Serena Williams whose every word and outfit carried weight. Systemic discrimination at that level seemed real, but I assumed it lived mostly in the spotlight, among women who had to think about respectability because they were, in effect, always on display.

What I found was much bigger. Dissemblance is very much real in today’s society, and it affects Black women of every age and social strata—not just celebrities. Take something as routine as going to the hospital: a Black woman can be stereotyped as having higher pain tolerance and as a less competent witness to her own body, even as clinicians undertreat her pain. At the same time, she faces overlapping expectations to perform composure, prove credibility, and avoid being read as angry or unprofessional the moment she advocates for herself—pressures that show up in classrooms, courtrooms, and workplaces alike.

Overall, progress is real in some places, but the distance between public narratives of advancement and what Black women report in clinics, classrooms, and courtrooms remains wide. I built a website rather than a paper alone so the images, timeline, and sources could carry evidence a linear essay would bury.

Looking Forward

Stop reading composure as consent; fix undertreatment, adultification, and punishment for testimony.

I don’t think this problem goes away on its own. If anything, doing this research made me see how easily the same patterns repeat—just in new settings, from courtrooms to hospitals to social media. But I also don’t think Black women have ever simply accepted those terms. From Harriet Jacobs to the Combahee River Collective to women speaking up in the #MeToo era, there has always been refusal alongside dissemblance. Progress is real in some places, yet the gap between how far we say we’ve come and what Black women actually live with every day is still huge.

On an individual level, the change I keep coming back to is pretty basic: stop treating a Black woman’s composure as proof that she is fine, and stop demanding that she perform respectability or trauma before you believe her. That showed up for me in our Anita Hill and Drew Dixon conversations, but also in everyday settings—like a Black woman trying to explain her pain in an ER without being treated like she is exaggerating or not credible. We can also stop framing disclosure as betrayal of the broader Black community when a Black man is involved. Listening without making someone choose between her story and her people’s dignity would go a long way.

Institutions have to carry weight individuals can’t. Hospitals need to stop undertreating Black women’s pain; schools need to stop treating Black girls as older or less innocent than they are; workplaces and courts need systems that don’t punish women for speaking up the way Anita Hill was punished. And honestly, history classes and public memory need to stop treating Black women’s stories as optional. Policy moves slowly, but an informed public can push faster. That is the hope I have for this website: if more people understand what dissemblance looks like in practice, maybe fewer people mistake silence for consent.
